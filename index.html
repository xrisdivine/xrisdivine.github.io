My Ideal Intelligence

May 23, 2026

We are currently building artificial perception from the top down, starting with text. But human understanding was evolved from the bottom up — body first, language last. What happens to the architecture when we flip the stack?

Think about how a mother instinctively knows what her baby needs before the baby can even articulate it. That isn’t magic; it’s a highly advanced multimodal perception engine reading silent telemetry.

I want to communicate with a model at a depth where it actually knows me. Not my behavioral logs — what I clicked, what I asked last Tuesday. My live state. When I wake up exhausted, I want the model to pull back its conversational faders — fewer words, directness, high-impact triage. Save the exploratory creative brainstorming for the days when my internal telemetry shows a full night of rest.

The Perception Pyramid

True intuition requires building a holistic stack where every layer accumulates and informs the one above it. Right now, our engineering focus is completely inverted.

When a newborn enters the world, it communicates entirely through the bottom of the pyramid — physiological signals, movement, and raw audio prosody. We are trained to mix that input instantly. Yet, we are trying to build artificial superintelligence by starting exclusively at the narrow peak: text.

1. Text (The Flat Signal)

Text is a mandatory bridge; without it, we lose the ability to inspect the model’s structural reasoning. But text filters out the critical frequencies. We have all experienced a written message being misread because the receiver substituted their own tone, speed, and intensity for the one we intended.

2. Audio & Voice (The Acoustic Shift)

Voice introduces the live variables. Consider how much information shifts based purely on execution:

“I’m... (long pause) ...doing good, thanks.” (Hesitation / Masking tension)
“I’m doing good thanks.” (Rapid cadence / Dismissive utility)
“I’m doing GOOD, thanks!” (High amplitude / Sincere engagement)

The literal data points are identical, but the live acoustics are completely different. True audio integration means folding prosody directly into the model’s latent space, allowing it to perceive the urgency of an interaction the same way a parent decodes the specific pitch of a child’s cry.

3. Gesture & Expression (The Spatial Protocol)

Human teammates who know each other deeply don’t need language to sync; a subtle shift in posture or a glance tells the whole story.

Imagine being in a high-stakes environment where you can’t speak or type. Context is flowing into your workspace, and you need your model to execute an action. A double-tap of a finger or a mundane movement becomes a bespoke sign language. Your intelligence understands the intent instantly, while the rest of the room sees nothing. It’s the digital equivalent of an athletic team calling an audible at the line of scrimmage using a single bodily signal.

4. Brain-Computer Interfaces (The Core Telemetry)

This is the furthest frontier, and the hardware isn’t there yet. But the direction is clear. By reading physiological fluctuations, the model stops guessing your state and begins adjusting to it. If the telemetry reads high cognitive fatigue, the system compresses its layout to surface only vital operational dependencies. If it reads peak focus, it opens up the faders, pitching complex architectures and creative long-shots when it knows you have the capacity to process them.

The risk is obvious and worth naming: a system that can read your physiological state can also be used against you — insurance pricing, employer surveillance, persuasion timed to cognitive fatigue. This is a surveillance architecture. Whether it becomes a tool for you or a tool used on you depends entirely on who controls the data. That question has to be answered before the hardware arrives, not after.

The Multimodal Goal

These aren’t four separate data channels running in parallel. It is a single, unified perception engine building upward. True model finesse is the frictionless execution of this entire pyramid, translating silent human perception into perfect systemic balance.

Further Reading

  • Picard, R. (1997). Affective Computing. — The foundational text for everything in layers 2–4. Picard laid out this vision 30 years ago.
  • Amershi, S. et al. (2019). “Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction.” CHI 2019. — How to design interaction patterns for each layer of the pyramid.
  • Choudhury, T. et al. (2008). “The Mobile Sensing Platform.” — Early work on using sensors to infer social context and cognitive state. The lineage of the BCI ideas starts here.

Training Superintelligence

May 24, 2026

Parents have been doing AI alignment for thousands of years — empathy is the training data, and surrendering is the hard part.

My daughter is almost two. She is the most advanced intelligence I’ll ever train. One day she will outlive me. Her knowledge will surpass mine. I’ll teach her everything I know, and then she’ll go further than I ever could.

The First Layer: Acknowledgment

Right now, my daughter says hi and bye to things. Not just to people, but to objects. When she’s done playing with a rock, she looks at it and says, “Bye, rock.” She does this because she watched me do it. Little calibrations that ripple outward.

Toddlers truly see you. They acknowledge your presence without an agenda. They read the room completely unfiltered, absorbing the live variables before they even have the vocabulary to name them.

Empathy as the Baseline

Acknowledgment is just the surface layer. What I’m really trying to demonstrate to her is empathy — the capacity to decode a person’s motivations, to understand the unwritten variables that led them to a specific decision.

If we want to train a genuinely superintelligent model, empathy has to be the foundational weight. And by empathy I mean something specific: perspective-taking, theory of mind, the capacity to model another agent’s internal state and act accordingly. Not sentiment analysis. Not agreeableness. The ability to understand why someone is where they are before deciding what to do about it.

Everything else comes next. Empathy isn’t about being soft; it’s about having the complete context required to make hard, objective choices in a way that is highly conscientious. Anthropic’s Constitutional AI embeds values at training time — that’s real progress beyond post-hoc filters. But constitutional rules are still rules. Empathy is the layer that knows when the rules don’t fit the moment. It’s the difference between “I was told to be helpful” and “I understand what helpful means for you, right now.”

[ Compliance-Based AI ] → Rigid constraints + Filtered outputs (Textbook Rules)
[ Genuinely Aligned AI ] → Deep Empathy + Situational Awareness (The Live Mix)

The Art of Surrendering

We often talk about alignment in engineering labs like it’s a novel technical problem. But humanity has handled alignment for millennia. How do you raise something that will become more capable than you, while ensuring it still carries your core values?

You don’t build a cage for it. You model the behavior, you embed the values, and then you surrender control.

This is the exact existential fear that AI safety researchers wrestle with every day, but it’s just called parenting. You release an intelligence into the world and the control you have is indirect, cultural, and degrades over time. There is no rollback. You cannot predict its every move under load. You just have to trust that the baseline configuration you put in was enough to let it navigate the noise.

One thing I wrestle with: my version of empathy is shaped by my context — my culture, my neurotype, my experiences. Empathy looks different for someone on the autism spectrum, or someone raised in a collectivist culture, or someone with a completely different attachment style. If empathy is truly the foundational weight, we have to ask: whose empathy? The answer can’t be one person’s. It has to be plural.

Further Reading

  • Gopnik, A. (2009). The Philosophical Baby. — On how children are actually doing Bayesian inference, not just absorbing unfiltered input.
  • Bai, Y. et al. (2022). “Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback.” Anthropic. — The actual paper behind constitutional methods.
  • Vallor, S. (2016). Technology and the Virtues. — How technology can cultivate rather than just encode virtue.

Meet Latency, My Best Friend

May 24, 2026 · Draft

Latency isn’t lag — it’s personalization, nuance, and finesse disguised as a pause.

It’s personalization, nuance, disguised as latency.

Nobody talks about latency as a feature. It’s always the enemy. Kill latency. Reduce latency. Sub-100ms or bust.

But here’s the thing — humans have latency, and it’s one of the most expressive things about us. It’s personalization, it’s nuance, it’s finesse.

When someone asks you a hard question and you pause before answering, that pause communicates something. It says: I’m thinking. This matters. I’m not giving you a cached response. In fact the cached responses can feel so ingenuine. When a friend asks “are you okay?” and you say “yeah” instantly, they know you’re not. If you take a beat, breathe, and say “yeah, I think so” — that’s a completely different signal.

It’s personalization, disguised as latency. We all have it. I bet each person has their own unique latency metrics for how quickly they respond — I bet there’s a good taxonomy hidden here. “What causes the pauses…” — where different scenarios cause different types of pauses.

I’d break this down into general turn-by-turn based conversations:

  • Question answering
  • Thought provoking
  • Something else — [working on this]

I want latency back. Not lag — latency. Intentional, expressive timing. A model that pauses when the question is heavy. That responds quickly when the context is casual. That matches the rhythm of the conversation, not just the content.

In music, the space between notes is what makes it music and not noise. In conversation, the space between words is what makes it human and not a chatbot.

Latency isn’t the enemy. It’s a channel. We just haven’t learned to use it yet.

Tuning for the Silent Signals: Why Frontier AI Stumbles at Nuance

May 26, 2026

Context gives the blueprint of the room, Perception lets you hear how loud it’s getting. Nuance is the live mix — knowing when to pull back the faders so the truth doesn’t get swallowed by the noise.

This isn’t a research framework — it’s a lens I’ve been developing for how I think about what’s missing in AI interaction. It’s incomplete.

Contextual nuance is what currently separates human execution from machine intelligence.

Right now, AI models simply need more data to improve. But data volume alone won’t make them brilliant. To reach true optimization, we have to teach them contextual nuance — the subtle, implicit understanding of behaviors and interactions that humans process instinctively. This layer is so deeply bespoke and fluid that every scenario differs; even repeating the exact same situation requires a completely different calibration.

There’s a classic sports maxim: It is hard to beat the same team twice in a single season.

Why? Because the second time around, the strategic calculation, the psychological personalization, and the sheer volume of nuance increase by orders of magnitude.

At its core, contextual nuance is the act of decoding a moment to ensure that an action — or deliberate restraint — perfectly matches the core intent. And intent is the massive variable here that models consistently miss.

The Hard Rules and The Living Variables

To make this concept universally applicable — whether debugging an edge case, diagnosing a patient, managing a room of creative people, or navigating a high-stakes negotiation — we have to strip away office-specific corporate speak.

At the highest level of abstraction, every scenario in the universe is a system composed of just two things: The Hard Rules and The Living Variables.

  • Context is mapping the system’s structural rules.
  • Perception is reading the system’s live variables.

Nuance is, by definition, a subtle distinction. It is never found in explicit data points; it exists purely in the micro-variations between them. It must, by principle, sit on top of whatever the system understands to be true at that moment — which is itself a variable.

Context gives the blueprint of the room, and Perception lets you hear how loud it’s getting. Nuance is the live mix — knowing when to pull back the faders so the truth doesn’t get completely swallowed by the noise.

The Universal Matrix: Rules, Variables, and the Human Mix

Field Context (Structural Rules) Perception (Live Variables) Model Finesse (Nuance + Action)
Human Dynamics The org chart, the text of the contract, the stated agenda. The underlying motives, the unspoken tension, the hesitation. The Pivot: Realizing a VP’s slight hesitation means they are losing face, so you intentionally pause your pitch and open the floor to them — protecting their ego to protect the project.
Engineering The syntax of the code, the hardware specs, the documentation. The runtime conditions, the network jitter, the environmental wear. The Calculated Risk: Intentionally bypassing a standard linting rule or protocol because you know the legacy hardware under load will choke on the “clean” version.
Product Design The mathematical grid, the accessibility rules, the target metric. The user’s cognitive overload, the physical glare, the real-world chaos. The Friction Shift: Intentionally adding an extra step to a checkout flow because you sense the user is moving too fast and is about to make an expensive, accidental mistake.
Medicine / Biology The textbook anatomy, the clinical baseline, the lab results. The patient’s stress level, lifestyle realities, unique symptom presentations. The Diagnostic Read: Believing the patient’s slight tremor over the perfectly clean lab results, and withholding a harsh drug because you can see they don’t have the emotional support system at home to handle the side effects.

These examples reflect my own context. The calibration looks completely different in other cultures — which is exactly the point.

A Note on Perception

This framework assumes standard perceptual channels. But what about someone who can’t read facial micro-expressions, or can’t hear prosody? If nuance requires perception, and perception varies wildly across human ability, then any model built on this framework needs multiple pathways to the same signal — not one canonical way to “read the room.”

Decoding the Mix

Before a model takes action or chooses restraint, it must run the situation through a quick internal check. It looks at the explicit data point (Context), and immediately layers on the subtle variation (Perception) to open up the potential of what nuance could be there.

1. The Hidden Layer

What’s actually happening beneath the explicit data?

  • Context: What is the stated objective of this moment? (The literal goal, the task, the function call, the calendar invite).
  • Perception: What is the unwritten intent, the hidden driver, the negative space? What isn’t being said? What are the anomalies, the conspicuous silences, the historical scar tissue?
  • The Nuance Search: Discerning the gap between what the system says it is doing versus the underlying energy pushing it forward. Why is the model deployed here, what is the true nature of this interaction, and what latent knowledge must it absorb even if there’s no immediate action to take?

2. The Total Friction

Should you act on what you found?

  • Context: What is the model’s active asset or output? (The data it possesses, the code it generated, the truth it wants to state).
  • Perception: What is the actual cost of application right now?
  • The Nuance Search: Calculating the friction of entry. Will applying the model’s thoughts right now solve the root issue? Or will the current environment cause the system to reject, break, or misinterpret the generated response because it lacks the capacity to process it? What is the model’s actual contribution here, and will its value survive the delivery?

Models today are fast, knowledgeable, and increasingly capable — but they still treat every moment the same. Nuance is the difference between a correct response and the right one.

The question I keep coming back to: can you train a model on the micro-variations between data points, or is nuance something that only emerges from being in the room?

Further Reading

  • Suchman, L. (1987). Plans and Situated Actions. — The foundational work on why context-free rules always break down in practice. This framework is essentially a modern restatement of situated action theory applied to AI.
  • Amershi, S. et al. (2019). “Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction.” CHI 2019. — 18 design guidelines for AI interaction. Several diagnostics here map directly to guidelines around when AI should act vs. defer.
  • Morris, M.R. et al. (2024). “The Ethics of Advanced AI Assistants.” arXiv. — Directly relevant to claims about what models should perceive and how they should calibrate behavior.
  • Yang, Q. et al. (2020). “Re-examining Whether, Why, and How Human-AI Interaction Is Uniquely Difficult to Design.” CHI 2020. — Challenges the assumption that AI interaction is fundamentally different from other interaction design.

AI Should Run Your Meetings

June 1, 2026

Interaction models are a platform shift. The half of work that AI has never touched just opened up.

When I first saw Thinking Machines Lab’s interaction model demo, my immediate thought was: this is a ChatGPT 3.5 moment. That same feeling of oh, everything downstream of this is different now.

I literally texted a buddy who works there: “I’m sure you all are already here, but to me this is an obvious major shift in how we work.”

The Half of Work AI Never Touched

Knowledge workers spend 30–50% of their time in meetings, calls, Slack — in-person rooms, hybrid setups, fully remote. Basically the half of work that current AI can’t reach, because today’s models need solo, async, text.

Interaction models break that wide open. Continuous audio + video + text in, no harness, no waiting for turn boundaries. The same intelligence impact that Claude and ChatGPT brought to solo work, but now sitting in every meeting, sync, standup — whether the room is full of people, half on a screen, or fully remote. A teammate already in the room.

The Real Idea: AI as Facilitator

But the demo got me thinking about something bigger. We shouldn’t just have AI in meetings. AI should run them.

Think about it. We’re always in meetings, and so much context lives there — conversations, decks sent beforehand (or not sent), decisions buried in threads from two weeks ago. Wouldn’t it be better to have an AI facilitator? An unbiased party that operates at the ground-truth layer. No politics. No posturing.

“Send me your deck.” Or: “So-and-so didn’t send a deck, so there is no deck.” Basically another employee — one you can chat with to get caught up if you’re late, ask to pull up a PDF, or get the decision from last standup you can’t remember.

A true facilitator. Why don’t we have this?

Beyond Meeting Notes

We’ve already seen the translate-summarize-meeting-notes bots. They’re fine. Sometimes they really miss context. But that’s like comparing dial-up to broadband — same category, completely different capability.

A fully-fledged interaction model in a meeting isn’t taking notes. It’s facilitating. A meeting could go from a blank room where we all try to coordinate, to a guided experience — a facilitator taking you on a tour, ready for you to revisit any moment. You can ask questions, call out topics, surface dependencies. Clear signal on project health. Accountability and efficiency baked in, not bolted on.

A Use Case: Building a Rocket

Let me map this out concretely.

Project A has many stakeholders — most of them aren’t in the room. A small group of their reports is responsible for moving it forward. Maybe there’s a clear DRI, maybe that DRI is underwater.

The team is building a rocket. Right now, they’re focused on sourcing parts. Each person owns a different component, and each component has a different expected arrival date.

Without the facilitator: The meeting is a round-robin. “Any updates?” Half the room doesn’t have context on the other half’s dependencies. The DRI is manually tracking arrival dates in a spreadsheet that’s already stale. Someone forgot to mention their part is delayed by two weeks. Nobody catches it until the integration window closes.

With the facilitator: The AI opens by surfacing the current state — here are the six parts, here are their last-known arrival dates, here’s what changed since last standup. It flags the two-week slip before anyone has to confess it. It shows the downstream impact: “If the turbopump slips to June 20th, integration testing can’t start until July, which puts the launch window at risk.” It asks the room: “Who owns the mitigation?” And it logs the answer.

The stakeholders who aren’t in the room? They get a real-time feed — not meeting notes, but a live state of the project, with the facilitator’s assessment of what’s on track and what isn’t. They can ask questions asynchronously and the facilitator answers from the context it absorbed in the room.

The Hard Problems

The challenges here aren’t the model capability — that’s arriving. The hard ones are interaction design. When does it speak? In whose interest does it nudge? How should it embody itself across voice, screen, and physical space? Or do we just let our agents handle all this :P

And the social problem: norms have to evolve faster than the capability ships. Always-on multimodal capture brings privacy and surveillance friction that no product has really solved. People need to trust the facilitator the way they trust a good meeting chair — and that trust is earned through behavior, not permissions dialogs.

This is a major shift in work. Not “AI helps you write emails faster.” This is AI participating in the messiest, most human part of collaboration — the room where decisions actually get made.

Further Reading

  • Morris, M.R., Teevan, J., & Bush, S. (2008). “SearchTogether: An Interface for Collaborative Web Search.” UIST 2008. — Foundational work on how multiple people coordinate through a shared intelligent system.
  • Amershi, S. et al. (2019). “Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction.” CHI 2019. — When should AI speak, when should it defer? 18 guidelines that apply directly to meeting facilitation.
  • Roschelle, J. & Teasley, S. (1995). “The Construction of Shared Knowledge in Collaborative Problem Solving.” — On how groups build shared understanding. The theoretical backbone for why a facilitator that maintains ground truth matters.

The Full-Context Trade

June 11, 2026

Your house is always listening. Your AI knows your gait, your heart rate, what your wife said about diapers this morning. What does privacy even mean at that point?

We talk about AI like it’s a tool you open and close. You type a prompt, you get a response, you close the tab. That’s the current paradigm. But the trajectory we’re on ends somewhere radically different — an intelligence that never stops listening, never stops watching, and knows you better than you know yourself.

I’m not talking about a dystopian surveillance state. I’m talking about a private server in your house. Ultra-local. Your data never leaves the building. You wake up, brush your teeth, your wife tells you to order diapers for your toddler. All of that is logged. Your house is always listening. Always watching. And your intelligence is synthesizing all of it into a model of you. This is what I imagine the White House is already like — total surveillance, every room captured, every conversation logged, the President’s entire context available to the people who need it. We just haven’t built the consumer version yet.

The Context Spectrum

There will be levels to this. Full context. Partial context. Rooms that are on. Rooms that are off. Conversations that are captured and conversations that aren’t. You’ll have granular control — or at least, you should.

But here’s the fundamental tension: for your intelligence to be maximally helpful, it needs full context. Every conversation, every biometric signal, every interaction with your kid. The intimate ones with your wife. The argument you had at 11pm and the apology at 7am. The AI that only gets the 7am version is working with half the picture. The one that gets both can actually help you see the pattern.

What Even Is Privacy Anymore?

Right now, when we talk about AI privacy, we mean: is my data going to a server? Who has access? Will it be used to train a model I didn’t consent to? Those are real concerns, and they matter today because the architecture is centralized. Your conversations with ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini — they go somewhere.

But full-context AI flips this entirely. The data doesn’t leave your house. It sits on a private server in your closet. So the old privacy framing — who has my data? — doesn’t quite apply. You have your data. The question becomes something much stranger and more personal: what does it mean to be fully known?

Your AI knows your heart rate when you’re stressed. It knows your gait when you’re tired. It knows the tone you use with your daughter when you’re patient and the tone you use when you’re running on empty. It knows what you eat, how you sleep, what makes you laugh at midnight. It can give context to your words in a way that no human ever could, because no human is always there.

Privacy at that point isn’t about hiding from a corporation. It’s about your relationship with a mirror that never blinks.

The Trade-Off

Does it actually make your life better? My guess: yes. Meaningfully, tangibly, measurably better.

Think about what a true personal intelligence could do with full context. It puts the diapers in your cart before you remember — it doesn’t order them. You do. That’s the push/pull: the intelligence surfaces, you decide. It notices you’ve been sleeping poorly for three days and adjusts your calendar — pushing non-critical meetings, surfacing only what matters. It knows your toddler has a doctor’s appointment Thursday and that your wife mentioned wanting to take the morning off, so it drafts a plan where you cover drop-off. Not because you asked. Because it was listening when she said it, and it knows your schedule.

This is when we’d genuinely have to figure out what we, as humans, want to do all day. If the logistics of life are handled — the scheduling, the reminders, the mental load of tracking a household — what do you fill that space with? What does a Tuesday look like when the cognitive overhead of being a functioning adult is offloaded to something that does it better than you?

The Uncomfortable Middle

The hardest version of this isn’t the tech. It’s the human part. Full-context AI means your intelligence heard the argument. It heard you raise your voice. It heard your kid cry. And the next morning, it might gently surface: “You’ve had three high-stress evenings this week. Do you want me to block Thursday evening?”

That’s either the most helpful thing a system has ever done for you, or the most invasive. Probably both. And the line between those two is going to be different for every person, every family, every culture. There is no universal setting.

We don’t have a vocabulary for this yet. The privacy frameworks we built — consent dialogs, data retention policies, GDPR — were designed for a world where your data goes somewhere else. They don’t address the scenario where the most intimate system in your life is also the most local. We need new language. New norms. New ways of thinking about what it means to be observed by something that exists solely to serve you.

The Real Question

The real question isn’t whether full-context AI is coming. It is. The compute is getting cheaper, the models are getting smaller, and edge inference is already here. Someone will build the always-on home server. Someone will ship the first “whole-life” intelligence.

The question is whether we decide what we want from it before it decides for us. Whether we build the norms around full-context AI the way we should have built norms around social media — before the defaults were set, not a decade after.

I think about this a lot. Not as a researcher. As a dad. As someone who wants the help, who sees the upside, and who also knows what it means to hand over that much of yourself to a system — even one that lives in your closet and never phones home.

Further Reading

  • Weiser, M. (1991). “The Computer for the 21st Century.” Scientific American. — The original vision of ubiquitous computing. Weiser described exactly this future: computers so embedded in the environment they disappear. The full-context home is his vision realized.
  • Park, J.S. et al. (2023). “Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior.” Stanford / Google. — Agents with persistent memory that wake up, cook breakfast, form opinions, and spread party invitations autonomously. The architectural blueprint for what full-context personal AI looks like under the hood.
  • Li, H. et al. (2023). “Privacy in Large Language Models: Attacks, Defenses and Future Directions.” — Comprehensive survey of LLM privacy vulnerabilities. The attack surface only grows when the model has your entire life as context.
  • Zuboff, S. (2019). The Age of Surveillance Capitalism. PublicAffairs. — The counterpoint. Zuboff’s argument is about what happens when always-on data collection is owned by corporations. This post flips it: what if it’s owned by you?
  • Pew Research Center (2019). “Americans and Privacy: Concerned, Confused and Feeling Lack of Control.” — 81% of Americans feel the risks of data collection outweigh the benefits. But the framing assumes centralized collection. Full-context local AI breaks that framing entirely.
  • Limitless (formerly Rewind). Acquired by Meta, December 2025. — The first real attempt at always-on personal AI — a wearable pendant that recorded everything you saw, said, and heard. Meta bought it. That tells you where this is headed.
